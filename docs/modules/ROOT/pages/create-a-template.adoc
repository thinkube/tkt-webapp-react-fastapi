= Turn your app into a template
:page-type: how-to
:page-docref: b6n1

There are two ways to get a template. Publish an app that already works, or write
one by hand. The first is the normal one.

== Publish an app you have built

. Get the app working and pushed. What you publish is the checkout at
  `~/apps/<name>`, so anything uncommitted is published too.
. In thinkube-control, open *Templates* and choose *Publish as Template* on your
  app.
. Give the template a name, a description and tags, and choose whether the
  repository is private.
. Publish. The platform creates the GitHub repository, pushes the code, and
  registers the template in the catalog so it appears for the next deployment.

Nothing is transformed on the way out, because nothing was substituted on the way
in.

=== What is left behind

These are dropped when your app is published:

[cols="1,2",options="header"]
|===
| Left behind | Why

| `k8s/` | Generated from `thinkube.yaml` at deploy time, and specific to your cluster.
| `.git` | The new repository gets its own history.
| `.copier-answers.yml` | The answers to your deployment, not the next one's.
| `.deployment-trigger` | A deploy marker.
| `node_modules`, `__pycache__`, `.venv` | Build and cache artefacts.
|===

=== What is carried, and should be

Your source, your tests, `thinkube.yaml`, `manifest.yaml`, and your
documentation. Also every *declaration* you added: the parameters your app asks
for, its `spec.env` defaults, its dependencies, its `publicEnv` allow-list. A
template that forgot what it needs would not deploy.

[IMPORTANT]
====
Values do not travel, but anything you hardcoded does. If you typed your
company's name into the source rather than reading it from a variable, the next
person's app is named after your company. See
xref:variables.adoc[How configuration reaches your app].
====

== Write a template by hand

A template is an application repository with two extra files at its root.

`manifest.yaml` describes the template in the catalog and declares what to ask at
deploy time:

[source,yaml]
----
apiVersion: thinkube.io/v1
kind: TemplateManifest
metadata:
  name: tkt-my-template
  title: My Application
  description: What it is, in one line
  version: 1.0.0
  author: Your Name
  tags: ["webapp", "fastapi"]

parameters: []
----

`thinkube.yaml` declares what the application *is* — its containers, how each is
built and tested, its routes and the platform services it needs:

[source,yaml]
----
apiVersion: thinkube.io/v1
kind: ThinkubeDeployment

spec:
  containers:
    - name: backend
      build: ./backend
      port: 8000
      health: /health
      test:
        enabled: true
        command: "./run_tests.sh"
        one: "./run_tests.sh <file>"

  routes:
    - path: /
      to: backend

  services:
    - database
----

The full field list is in the xref:thinkube-docs:reference/thinkube-yaml.adoc[thinkube.yaml reference].

Then:

. Give every container a health endpoint. The platform uses it to decide whether
  the app is up.
. Give every container a `run_tests.sh` that runs the whole suite with no
  arguments and one file with a file argument. Keep the test settings in that
  script, not spread across `thinkube.yaml`.
. Use `+${CONTAINER_REGISTRY}+` for base images in your Dockerfiles, so the
  template works on any cluster.
. Write no placeholders into the source. Read what varies from the environment.
. Push to GitHub and add the repository to `thinkube-metadata` so it appears in
  the catalog.

== Check it round-trips

The test of a template is not that it deploys. It is that an app deployed from
it, improved and published back, produces a template you would deploy again.
Before you publish, look for your own app's identity in the source: its name in
`package.json`, in a page title, in a heading. Each one is a value that should
have been a variable.
